Running out of time

Australia’s threatened species

Australia has more than 2,000 plants and animals on the national threatened species list. This page follows them from the national list to the bush, then asks a harder question: is anyone counting them?

Every species on the national list, 2,222 in all, from left to right: Vulnerable 35% Endangered 39% Critically Endangered 21% Conservation Dependent (7 species) and Extinct 5%.

Part 1 of 5

How bad is it?

Under national law, a species is listed when scientists agree it is at risk of disappearing. Today that is 2,119 species, from 49 frogs to nearly 1,500 plants. Another 103 have already gone for good, including 39 mammals.

The list ranks risk in three tiers: Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered, meaning an extremely high risk of extinction in the wild. Four in ten invertebrates (43%) and nearly four in ten amphibians (37%) are in that top tier, compared with fewer than one in ten mammals (8%).

Part 2 of 5

Where do they live?

New South Wales has the longest list, with 732 species. Most threatened species live in one state only, so each state mostly carries its own load. Per square kilometre the picture flips: tiny Tasmania, Victoria and the ACT are far more crowded than WA or the NT.

Part 3 of 5

Is it getting better?

Scientists track thousands of wild populations through the Threatened Species Index. By 2019 the average monitored population had fallen to about a quarter of its 1985 level. Since then it has edged up to 26% in 2022, but the uncertainty is wide enough that it is too early to call a recovery.

Frogs and reptiles have fared worst, falling to about 4% and 6% of their 1985 levels. Mammals look better, but the shaded band is very wide, so the true picture is uncertain.

Part 4 of 5

Who is watching?

Only 16% of listed species are tracked in the Index. More than half of listed mammals and amphibians are included, but just 1 in 9 plants. Fish and invertebrates are not in the Index at all.

On this page, monitored means tracked in the Index. Other surveys may follow some of these species, but the Index is the national picture of how threatened populations are changing.

Part 5 of 5

What now?

Australia's threatened species are a story of loss and of blind spots. A hundred and three species are gone for good, and the populations we do follow have fallen to about a quarter of their 1985 size, with frogs and reptiles hit hardest.

Yet only 16% of listed species are tracked in the Index, and the most endangered are barely more likely to be counted than those that are merely vulnerable (17% against 15%). Fish and invertebrates are missing altogether. Without regular counts, it is hard to tell whether protection is working.

A clearer picture would start with monitoring that follows the threat: the most endangered species first, and the groups we cannot see at all.
